FINS

How do fins change the way a surfboard feels?

Different fin configurations

Thruster — Three fins. The familiar all-round setup on a huge number of modern boards.

Twinny — Two fins. Usually looser and faster, with less hold than a thruster.

Quad — Four fins. Can give you speed and drive without a centre fin.

Single fin — One larger centre fin. Common on longboards and some mid-lengths, with a very different feel from a thruster or quad.

Five-fin setup — A board with five boxes gives you the option of changing between different configurations.

There are plenty of variations, but you don't need to understand all of them before you start surfing.

Shape matters

Fin shape changes how the board feels. A more upright template can make a board feel quicker to turn, while a longer, more swept-back fin can give more hold and drive through a turn. Bigger fins generally give more hold and stability, while smaller fins can feel looser.

That's a general guide rather than a rule. The board, surfer and conditions all matter.

Flex matters too

Some fins are stiff and some have considerably more flex. That changes the way they load up and release through a turn. Different materials and constructions are used to create those differences.

So what should a beginner buy?

Usually, the fins that came with the board are a perfectly good place to start. If you're still learning, there is probably more to gain from catching more waves than from spending a fortune experimenting with fins.

Once you know how your board feels, changing fins can be a useful way to fine-tune it. That's when size, template and flex start getting more interesting.

What about FCS, FCS II and Futures?

This is the boring but important bit. The fin needs to fit the box in your board. Futures uses a different base from the FCS systems. FCS I uses the familiar two-tab fin, while FCS II uses a different insertion system.

But there's a useful bit of compatibility that is easy to miss: FCS I fins can be used in FCS II boxes with the appropriate compatibility inserts and grub screws. So an FCS II board doesn't necessarily make an existing collection of FCS I fins useless.

The reverse is different. An FCS II fin isn't simply a drop-in replacement for an FCS I box. Check the board and fin before buying rather than assuming the names mean they're interchangeable.

FCS II without the screws?

FCS II fins are designed to click into the box without screws, which is handy. But if you've got an expensive set of fins, adding the grub screws isn't a bad idea. A hard knock can loosen a fin or send it on its own little adventure, and you don't want to lose an expensive fin halfway through a surf.

It's also worth having a small pack of spare grub screws and a fin key in the board bag. An FCS multi-tool is handy too, particularly when a fin screw is being stubborn or you're changing fins at the beach.

Useful bits to keep around: FCS I/FCS II compatibility inserts, spare grub screws, a fin key or multi-tool, and the right fins for your board. These are small purchases compared with replacing a lost fin.
